Replies to Adam Bolton's Comments

1. Global population hyperparameterization vs explicit pairing
Reply: While a global population approach is a highly valuable technique, we chose to focus on explicit geometric pairing to strictly isolate the distance-ratio cosmology and preserve the traditional DSPL formalism. The main advantage of this approach is that we only care about the relative differences between paired deflectors, which allows us to directly leverage the tight scaling relations of massive ellipticals to suppress scatter. A global population approach would require modeling the full distribution of all observables and their covariances, which is a much more complex problem. Thus, we maintain the pairing approach in the text.

2. Systematic floor for PDSPL cosmology
Reply: We have added a new section, Section 6.4 (Systematic Uncertainties), which explicitly lists the systematic errors this method is susceptible to, including photometric-to-mass mapping, environmental variations, photo-z evolution, and selection bias. Because we only care about relative differences between paired deflectors, many systematic errors that affect absolute mass calibration are largely mitigated.

Regarding the systematic floor, while a rigorous derivation requires full forward modeling, we estimate it to be ~1% to 2% on beta_E. This is primarily driven by unmodeled differences in external convergence (e.g., ~1% scatter per Johnson et al. 2025) and redshift-dependent evolution in the photometric-to-mass mapping. However, the massive statistical volume of the PDSPL framework allows us to introduce nuisance parameters for these effects. By self-calibrating and marginalizing over them during the hierarchical inference, we can effectively trade a hard systematic bias for a manageable increase in statistical uncertainty which would probably still be much smaller compared to the dissimilarity scatter in beta_E.

3. Anisotropic Dissimilarity Weighting and Covariances
Reply: In Section 6.2, we expanded the weighting discussion to explicitly note that observables like effective radius and magnitude possess significant intrinsic covariances, proposing a generalized Mahalanobis-like distance metric for future iterations.

*4. Pairing Logic (Are some left unpaired / Multiple pairings?)
Reply: Our kD-Tree algorithm queries the nearest neighbor for *every* individual deflector, meaning no deflector is left completely unpaired. However, because nearest-neighbor matches are not always reciprocal (e.g., Galaxy A matches B, but C also matches A), the algorithm naturally allows for multiple pairings of the same galaxy. This is beneficial as it maximizes our total statistical sample size. 

Regarding the gain from excluding poor pairs: instead of strictly excluding them, our likelihood function naturally down-weights them. It does this by parameterizing their variance ($\sigma_{\beta_E, \mathcal{D}}$) as a function of their dissimilarity ($\mathcal{D}$), allowing us to use the full dataset without being overwhelmed by noisy matches.

5. Realistic relationship between mass and light parameters in simulation
Reply: Author's Reply: 
Yes, the simulation employs highly realistic, empirically calibrated relationships. As implemented in the SLSim framework (Khadka et al. 2026), our mock deflector catalogs assign mass parameters (such as central velocity dispersion) to photometric profiles using SDSS-calibrated abundance matching and established scaling relations (e.g., the $L-\sigma$ relation). Furthermore, the photometric and structural parameters are drawn from observationally constrained distributions via the SkyPy pipeline, ensuring that the simulated population accurately reflects the intrinsic parameter relationships observed in real galaxy surveys.

6. Selection bias (minimum Einstein radius threshold)
Author's Reply: Thank you for highlighting this important point; this is now explicitly discussed in our new Section 6.4 (Systematic Uncertainties). We note that survey detectability criteria indeed create a Malmquist-like bias by preferentially selecting systems that scatter upward in deflection angle. We already see this effect in our simulated populations: for 4MOST-based samples where we strictly select bright deflector galaxies, the intrinsic scatter is lower (~10%) compared to the broader case where we supply LSST Y10 with spec-z's (~15%, see Appendix B), indicating that selection cuts directly influence the pairing fidelity.

As for the cosmological inference, because the PDSPL observable ($\beta_E$) is fundamentally a ratio of two Einstein radii, an absolute upward scatter in mass might partially cancel out between a paired deflector. However, this cancellation is not perfect. The minimum $\theta_E$ detectability threshold affects systems differently depending on their source redshifts—for instance, a lens with a lower source redshift requires a more extreme upward scatter in its mass profile to clear the threshold than an identical deflector with a higher source redshift. 

This differential selection effect introduces a redshift-dependent bias that systematically skews the inferred population mass parameters (such as the mean power-law slope and MST parameter) which directly propagates into the cosmological constraints. While we do not explicitly attempt to correct for this bias in our current simulation (as our forecast utilizes Asimov datasets to establish the theoretical statistical bounds of the methodology), we have explicitly noted in Section 6.4 that rigorous forward-modeling of the survey selection function will be a mandatory requirement when applying this framework to observational data, much like it currently is for single-lens cosmography and time-delay measurements.

7. Quantifying "1 spec pair is worth X phot pairs"
Reply: One way to quantify this is to compare the irreducible scatter in beta_E for the photometric scenario vs the spectroscopic scenario. 
For N_photo pairs and N_spec pairs, the effective scatter for each scenario is:
sigma_eff_photo = sigma_photo / sqrt(N_photo)
sigma_eff_spec = sigma_spec / sqrt(N_spec)
To achieve the same effective scatter, we can set sigma_eff_photo = sigma_eff_spec, which gives:
N_photo / N_spec = (sigma_photo / sigma_spec)^2 ~ (0.19 / 0.10)^2 ~ 3.27
Thus, roughly speaking, 1 spec pair is worth about 3.27 photometric pairs in terms of reducing the effective scatter in beta_E.
This is just an approximate quantification as dissimilarity scatter contributes most of the noise floor, but it gives a rough sense of the relative value of spectroscopic pairs.
